Computers count in powers of two, people count in powers of ten, and estimates live in the gap between them. Knowing that 2¹⁰ is about a thousand and 2³² is about four billion lets you size key spaces, memory and storage in your head. It also helps you notice when a "1 TB" disk shows up as 931 "GB".

<Aside type="tip" title="TL;DR">
- 2¹⁰ ≈ 10³. Every ten powers of two add about three zeros: 2²⁰ ≈ a million, 2³⁰ ≈ a billion, 2⁴⁰ ≈ a trillion.
- 2³² ≈ 4.3 billion (IPv4 addresses, unsigned 32-bit integers); 2³¹ − 1 ≈ 2.1 billion is the classic signed-integer overflow.
- **KB, MB, GB** are powers of 1,000. **KiB, MiB, GiB** are powers of 1,024. The gap grows from 2.4% at kilo to 10% at tera.
- For estimates, round freely: treat 2¹⁰ as 1,000 and say so.
</Aside>

## KiB vs KB

Two families of prefixes describe bytes, and they are often mixed up:

| Decimal (SI) | Value | Binary (IEC) | Value | Binary is larger by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| kilobyte, KB | 10³ = 1,000 | kibibyte, KiB | 2¹⁰ = 1,024 | 2.4% |
| megabyte, MB | 10⁶ | mebibyte, MiB | 2²⁰ = 1,048,576 | 4.9% |
| gigabyte, GB | 10⁹ | gibibyte, GiB | 2³⁰ | 7.4% |
| terabyte, TB | 10¹² | tebibyte, TiB | 2⁴⁰ | 10% |
| petabyte, PB | 10¹⁵ | pebibyte, PiB | 2⁵⁰ | 12.6% |

The IEC introduced the binary names in 1999 to end the ambiguity, but both meanings of "GB" are still in use:

- **Drive makers, network speeds and most cloud storage prices use decimal units.** A "1 TB" drive holds 10¹² bytes, and 1 Gbit/s is 10⁹ bits per second.
- **Memory is sized in binary units**, even when the label says "GB": a 16 GB DIMM holds 16 GiB.
- **Operating systems differ.** Windows reports binary sizes with decimal labels, which is why a 1 TB disk appears as "931 GB". macOS uses decimal units. Linux tools vary: `ls -lh` and `df -h` print powers of 1,024 with bare K, M, G suffixes, while `df -H` prints powers of 1,000.
- **Cloud services choose either.** Some bill block storage per GiB-month, others per GB-month; check the docs when a 7% difference matters.

<Aside type="note">
Bits and bytes are a separate trap: network bandwidth is quoted in **bits** per second, storage in **bytes**. Divide by 8 (or by 10 to leave room for protocol overhead): a 10 Gbit/s link moves about 1 GB/s of payload.
</Aside>

## Why it matters in a design

| Question | Powers of two at work |
|---|---|
| How many IDs fit in a 32-bit or 64-bit integer? | 2³² ≈ 4.3 × 10⁹ runs out at web scale; 2⁶⁴ ≈ 1.8 × 10¹⁹ does not. |
| How long must a short URL key be? | Base62 with 7 characters gives 62⁷ ≈ 3.5 × 10¹² keys (about 2⁴²): plenty for billions of links issued by a counter; with random keys retries grow as the space fills, so the [URL shortener](/practice/url-shortener-pastebin/) moves to 8 characters at tens of billions. |
| Will random IDs collide? | The birthday bound: collisions become likely after about `2^(n/2)` random n-bit IDs, so about 4 billion for 64-bit IDs. A random UUID has 122 random bits. |
| How much memory does an index take? | 100 million entries × 64 bytes = 6.4 × 10⁹ bytes ≈ 6 GiB: one large server, not a cluster. |
| How many shards or partitions? | Powers of two split evenly: going from 2ᵏ to 2ᵏ⁺¹ shards splits each shard in two and moves about half of its keys. |
| How big is a page or block? | Memory pages are usually 4 KiB, database pages 8–16 KiB, SSD reads 4 KiB, object-storage multipart parts at least 5 MiB. |

When you estimate, keep units visible all the way through: "10⁸ objects × 1 KB = 10¹¹ bytes = 100 GB". Writing the exponent makes slips of a factor of 1,000 obvious.

<Aside type="note" title="In the interview">
Interviewers use powers of two to see whether you can size things quickly: "will the key space run out?", "does this fit in RAM?". Say the approximation you use ("I'll treat 2¹⁰ as a thousand"), keep units on every number and round to one significant figure. Confusing bits with bytes, or MB with Mb, is the most common slip; being off by 7% between GB and GiB almost never matters.
</Aside>
